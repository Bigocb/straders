Startraders · command UI · design review

The player has no loop

The engine mines, hauls, trades and rescues itself. The interface renders all of it faithfully. What neither of them does is give the person watching a reason to be there.

A game-design read of the current interface, and two directions it could go instead.
The diagnosis

The engine has a loop. The player doesn't.

Look at what the player actually does, minute to minute. They open the page. They watch a credit counter go up. Then nothing — because nothing is ever asked of them.

Every good management game runs on a supervisory loop: the system produces a situation, the situation demands a judgement, the judgement produces feedback, and the feedback teaches you to make a better system. Factorio calls this outgrowing your factory. RimWorld calls it a raid. What they share is that the automation is never the end state — it's the thing that generates the next decision.

Startraders has the automation and stops there. Credits rise on their own. Nothing threatens them. Nothing is scarce that the player controls. No decision is ever forced, and no decision is ever scored. That isn't a UI bug — you can wire every button on this page correctly and it will still be a screensaver.

The interface's real job is not to display the fleet. It's to manufacture the moments where a human judgement beats the engine's default — and to make the outcome of that judgement legible afterwards.

The good news is that the raw material for that loop already exists in the codebase, unused. The engine knows when a ship is stranded. It knows why a ship went idle. It computes a goal for every ship on every tick. It keeps a full ledger. All of that is either thrown to console.log or flattened into a single number in the topbar.

Credit where due

What's already right

The aesthetic is a real one. Amber on void, a condensed display face against mono data, the serif captain's log — it commits to a world. Most hobby dashboards look like Bootstrap. This doesn't.

The map is genuinely well-built. Constant stroke weights under zoom and labels that reveal at high magnification are the correct techniques, done properly.

The room metaphor is a good instinct. A mining desk and a trading desk should not offer the same controls. The idea is sound; it's the execution that stalled.

The co-pilot has a voice. "You call the human operator captain." Someone thought about character. That's an asset most projects don't have.

Where it breaks down

Six failures of play, not of code

These are all things that would still be wrong if every endpoint were wired perfectly.

No feedback

The score is a level, not a rate

The topbar reads 412,500. That number tells the player nothing, because it only ever goes up and it goes up whether or not they did anything. In a management game the derivative is the score. Did my last decision help? Currently unanswerable.

Fix → Show credits per hour, trending against the last few hours, and attribute it per ship. "412,500 · +18.2k/hr · best hour yet" turns the counter into a verdict on your last change.
No stakes

Nothing is ever at risk

You cannot lose. Ships strand occasionally, and the engine dispatches a fuel tender to fix it before you notice. Without a threat there's no tension, and without tension there is no reason to open the tab.

Fix → The stake in a fleet game is opportunity cost, and it's already computable. Price idleness out loud: "3 ships idle · 4,200c/hr forgone." Give the engine's auto-rescue a visible countdown so intervening early is a real choice rather than a redundant one.
No agency

Interventions are unpriced bets

Move, Refuel, Buy, Scout — each is a button with no stated cost and no stated consequence. A decision the player can't evaluate isn't a decision, it's a dice roll, and players stop pressing buttons whose effects they can't perceive.

Fix → Price every action inline. "Move to C3 — 34 fuel (~2,400c), 4m transit, interrupts a mining cycle worth 6,100c." The dispatch picker already shows "(89 fuel)" — that's the pattern; generalise it to every control.
No comparison

Ships are interchangeable

Every card shows symbol, status, role, waypoint. None shows how the ship is doing. Which miner is carrying the fleet? Which has been parked for an hour? A fleet game where units can't be compared has no fleet decisions in it — only ship decisions, taken blind.

Fix → Per-ship earnings over the last hour, idle time, and the engine's current goal in plain words. ShipGoal is already computed every tick and never leaves the process.
Wrong shape

Rooms fragment the one question that matters

Seven rooms, each a click away, none visible from the others. But the actual job — "which of my ships is underperforming?" — is cross-cutting. Answering it today means visiting four rooms and comparing from memory. The metaphor serves the fiction and fights the work.

Fix → Either make one surface answer the cross-fleet question (see The Bridge), or drop the pretence that the player is an operator at all (see The Long Watch).
Orphaned asset

The fiction is quarantined in a tab

The captain's log, the co-pilot's dry voice, the room names, "telemetry live" — the whole fictional layer is well-written and lives in a sidebar panel most sessions never open. Worse, the LLM narrator that would make the log genuinely alive is fully implemented and never called; the endpoint serves the templated fallback instead.

Fix → Decide whether the fiction is the frame or the garnish. If it's the frame, it can't be a tab. At minimum, make the co-pilot proactive: "Captain — IRON_ORE just spiked at A1. Want AG-3 rerouted?" An advisor that only answers when asked is a search box with a personality.
Path A · incremental

Making what you have better

Keeping the room model intact, in descending order of what it buys you per hour of work.

  1. Put a rate in the topbar

    Credits per hour with a trend arrow and a sparkline. One change, and every other decision in the app acquires a scoreboard to be judged against.

  2. Turn Overview into a bridge

    An alert queue ranked by cost-of-inaction, above a single table of every ship with earnings and idle time. The cross-fleet question gets one place to live without dismantling the rooms.

  3. Surface the engine's reasoning

    "No mining target in range." "Waiting on survey." "Holding — can't reach a market on this tank." The engine already says these things to a log file nobody reads. In the UI they become the game's diagnostic layer.

  4. Price every button

    Cost, duration, and what it interrupts — inline, before the click.

  5. Animate the map along routes

    Ships currently sit at their origin until they teleport on arrival, because position comes from waypointSymbol. nav.route carries departure and arrival times — interpolating between them turns a static chart into a picture of a working fleet.

  6. Wire the narrator, make the co-pilot speak first

    Both are built. Neither is connected. This is the cheapest character work available.

The choice

You can't have both

The Bridge maximises information and agency. The Long Watch maximises atmosphere and meaning-per-decision. Every pixel that serves one is taken from the other — density is the enemy of mood, and mood is the enemy of scanning.

The current design is the honest evidence of trying to have both: a beautiful map that carries no information, a written narrator nobody reads, seven rooms that fragment the only question worth asking. It's not that the work was bad. It's that the brief was never settled.

If you don't want to choose yet, the incremental path is safe and buys real ground — the rate in the topbar alone changes how every other part of the app feels. But it defers the question rather than answering it. My read, for what it's worth: The Long Watch is the more distinctive game and plays better to what you've already built well — the voice, the log, the aesthetic. The Bridge is the better tool, and the one you'll want if the pleasure you're actually chasing is watching a number you tuned go up.
