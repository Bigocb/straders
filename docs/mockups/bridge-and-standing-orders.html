Bridge or Standing Orders

Two design poles for the command centre, and the synthesis of them — all built for a desktop browser and populated with a realistic seven-ship fleet. Start with C.

You said you want stakes and things to do, that you like tinkering with the fleet, and that you love automation. That last combination is the real design constraint, so it's worth being explicit about what it rules out and what it changes.

What your brief changed The Long Watch is out. As I originally pitched it — two minutes twice a day, one written choice, no direct control — it's the opposite of tinkering. So the second option is rebuilt around your actual constraint: you still don't fly ships one at a time, but instead of watching, you tune the doctrine the engine flies by, and every rule is scored on what it earned or cost. Same automation-first spirit, but it gives you something to fiddle with and a way to be wrong.

A · The Bridge — you tinker with ships

One screen, no navigation. The engine flies everything; your job is to notice the handful of situations where a human beats its defaults, and to act before the cost accrues. Triage is ranked by credits-per-hour of inaction, which is what turns a notification list into a game — the top item is always the one costing you most right now.

The stakes are real but never fatal: each alert shows what the engine will do on its own and when, so ignoring it is a legitimate choice with a visible price. The contract-at-risk card is the sharpest case — the engine has no plan for it, so it's pure player judgement.

Tinkering lives in the fleet table: role is a dropdown, so you're not steering hulls one at a time, you're reassigning them and letting the autonomy re-plan. Ships are sorted by earnings, so your worst performer is always in view.

B · Standing Orders — you tinker with the automation

You never touch a ship. You write the doctrine the engine flies by, then read what it produced and adjust. Every rule carries its own P&L, so the doctrine panel is a scoreboard as much as a settings screen — the margin floor made 22,100c this shift, the growth rule is 210,000c underwater and says so.

The stakes come from being wrong in a way you can measure. "Unruled losses" is the sharpest number on the screen: 1,700c bled through gaps your doctrine doesn't cover, which is the game telling you where to write the next rule. The disabled contract rule showing "would have refused the iron contract now at risk" is the same idea pointed backwards.

Decisions still arrive, but the third button is the one that matters: Make scuttling doctrine turns a one-off call into a standing rule, so the fiddling compounds instead of repeating. That's the automation-lover's loop — you're not playing the game, you're improving the thing that plays it.

C · All three, ranked

The eight-room problem was never too many surfaces — it was peer surfaces with no hierarchy, so nothing was more important than anything else and the cross-fleet question had nowhere to live. Three ranked views is a different shape: Bridge is where you sit, Doctrine is where you go when something has happened twice, Markets is where you go to answer a question.

The Markets view above is the one you haven't seen — it's your current dashboard's reference data, kept whole, but with the routes list fixed. Today bestTrades() ranks by margin percentage, which is why IRON_ORE at 111% looks like the best trade in the game when it's three units of volume 41 fuel away. Ranking by profit per trip net of fuel puts CLOTHING on top, where it belongs, and the callout explains the difference rather than hiding it.

What stops this collapsing back into eight rooms is the wiring above the mockup. Each view hands off to the next with state attached, so a situation becomes a rule becomes a question and back again — one loop across three surfaces, rather than three apps sharing a topbar.

What each one costs to build

The three views are not equally expensive, and that should drive the order more than taste does.

Markets · cheapestAlmost entirely a re-rank and re-layout of data you already store. The snapshots, price history, shipyard inventory and module catalog are all in SQLite today. The only real work is replacing margin-percentage ranking with profit-per-trip net of fuel.
Bridge · moderateThe map, ledger, stranded detection and per-ship goals all exist. What's missing is per-ship earnings attribution and a cost-of-inaction score to rank triage by — both derivable from the ledger you already keep, but neither is computed today.
Doctrine · mostNeeds a rule engine that does not exist: rules the coordinator evaluates each tick, plus attribution so every rule can carry its own P&L. That attribution is the hard half — it's what makes the panel a scoreboard instead of a settings screen, and it's the whole point.

So the order writes itself: Markets first, because it is nearly free and immediately fixes advice that is currently wrong in both the UI and the co-pilot. Then Bridge, which is what makes the fleet legible and gives you the rate to judge everything else by. Then Doctrine, once you've spent time in triage and know from experience which decisions you keep making — because the rules worth writing are the ones you've already made by hand three times.

Building them in that order also means each stage stands on its own. Markets is useful without Bridge. Bridge is useful without Doctrine. Nothing is stranded half-built if you stop.
